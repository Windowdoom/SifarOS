THE UNFINISHED

four stories that refuse to end
None of these stories will tell you what happened. That is not a flaw. A story that ends can be closed, shelved, survived. These are left open so that you will finish them yourself — tonight, most likely, somewhere between turning off the light and falling asleep.

The Counting

I

Maren counted her children every night. It was not a ritual of fear — it was what her own mother had done, standing in the doorway with the hall light behind her, tapping the air over each sleeping head. One. Two. Three. A small arithmetic of love. She had done it every night for eleven years and it had never once been interesting.

On the fourth of November she counted four.

She did not scream. That was the thing she could never explain afterward, to herself, in the versions of that night she rehearsed while washing dishes: she stood in the doorway of the long attic room and counted four sleeping shapes where three children ought to be, and her body simply declined to panic. The way it declines to panic in dreams. She counted again, slowly, touching each name as she went. Isa by the window. Little Tom against the wall. Bee in the middle, mouth open. And between Bee and Tom, where there had never been a bed — a bed. With a child in it. Sleeping the way her children slept, one arm thrown up over the head, entirely at home.

Every instinct said which one is extra — and this was the moment Maren later could not think past, because she went to look, and there was no extra one. Each face, in the low amber of the hall light, was a face she had kissed ten thousand times. Each had a smell she knew, a scar she remembered the day of. Four children. All hers. All, her heart said with terrible serenity, always hers.

She went downstairs and took the photo album from the shelf with hands that were almost steady. Birthdays. Beaches. The kitchen in summer. Three children in every photograph — of course, of course — but she stared at the beach one until the print seemed to grain apart, because she could not for her life have said which three. The faces in the pictures were her children's faces. So was the fourth one upstairs. The album told her only what she already knew: that she had three children, and had four.

· · ·

She was still at the kitchen table when the stairs creaked, around two, the particular fourth stair that always announced someone small coming down for water. She listened to the feet cross the hall. She did not turn around. The refrigerator opened; the milk was lifted out; a cup was filled with the practiced sounds of a child who had done this in this kitchen every night of their life.

"Mummy," said a voice she knew completely and could not name, "you always miss one."

Maren turned around then. The kitchen was empty. The cup stood on the counter, full, and upstairs — she took the stairs two at a time with her heart finally, finally hammering — upstairs in the long attic room under the sloped roof she stood in the doorway and tapped the air over each sleeping head, and counted, and there were three, thank God, thank God, three children, Isa and Tom and Bee, three beds, three, exactly as it had always—

From the far end of the attic, from the dark past the wardrobe where the roof came down to meet the floor, a small voice said, patiently, a little muffled, the way it sounds when a child has been hiding a very long time and has been very good about it:

"Am I done hiding now?"

— Maren is still standing at the top of the stairs. So far as anyone knows, she has not yet answered.

Eleven Minutes

II

The voicemail arrived at nine in the evening, which was strange, because his phone had not rung. Stranger was the caller: his own number, his own name, the little photograph of himself smiling at some barbecue two summers ago. Strangest of all was the timestamp the phone insisted on, in small grey type, with the boundless confidence of machines: Tonight, 3:11 a.m.

It was nine. The message was from six hours in the future.

Daniel played it, because you would too. Three seconds of room tone — a room, he thought, very like his own bedroom, the same faint tick from the same radiator. And then his own voice. Not distressed. That was the dreadful part, in retrospect: how calm he sounded, how rehearsed, like a man who has had a long time to think about exactly one sentence and has stripped everything else away.

"Don't open the door for me."

That was the message in its entirety. He played it eleven times. On the twelfth, the phone reported no such voicemail, and never had.

· · ·

He did the reasonable things. Checked the locks — twice, then a third time, hating himself. Left the hallway light on. Sat up in bed with the phone face-up on the blanket, watching its clock the way you watch a kettle, and the clock, in the way of clocks after midnight, poured itself out: one, then two, then the long grey trench of the small hours. At three he stopped pretending and went and stood in the hallway, facing the front door, arms folded, feeling absurd. 3:04. The radiator ticked. 3:07.

The knock was soft. Almost apologetic. Three taps, the knock of someone who knows the house sleeps.

"Dan." His own voice, through the door. Not calm — this was the difference, and his skin understood it before he did — this voice was ragged, winded, a voice that had been running. "Dan, it's me. Listen to me very carefully. Something is wearing my voice. It got into the phone — it left you a message so you wouldn't open the door — that's how it works, it makes you afraid of the right thing at the wrong moment — Dan, I am begging you, it is right behind me, LET ME IN—"

And the hallway was silent, except that behind the door someone was crying, quietly, the way he cried, the exact and private way he had cried at his father's funeral when he'd thought no one could hear, a way of crying that no one alive had ever heard.

The phone in his hand said 3:10.

He looked down at it. The calm voice had been recorded in a quiet room, by a man with all the time in the world. The voice behind the door had no time at all. One of them was his. One of them had had six hours to practice.

The phone said 3:11.

Daniel put his hand on the latch.

— The message, remember, had already been sent. The only question is by which of them, and the door knows the answer by now. You don't.

Thirteen Steps

III

The hallway of the Vasey house was twelve footsteps long. Nora knew this the way children know the true dimensions of a house — not in metres but in journeys: twelve steps from her bedroom door to the bathroom, heel to toe, which she had counted a hundred times on a hundred ordinary nights, because counting was what you did in the dark to keep the dark polite.

In the last week of August it was thirteen.

She counted twice, stood in the bathroom doorway frowning back down the length of it, and decided her feet had grown lazy in the summer, dragging shorter steps. Children are magnificent at deciding such things. The next night it was thirteen again, and the night after that, fourteen, and she began taking bigger steps, deliberately, absurdly, stretching her stride like a soldier — fourteen — and the hallway accepted her effort with what she could only describe, later, as patience.

By day it was twelve steps. She checked, feeling foolish, sunlight lying flat and honest on the floorboards. Her mother carried laundry down it; her father whistled through it; twelve. The hallway kept its true length for the daylight the way certain people keep their true face for strangers.

Fifteen. Sixteen. She stopped drinking water after supper so she would not have to make the journey, and lay rigid instead, listening to the house settle — except that settling, she now noticed, had a rhythm to it, and the rhythm was footsteps, and they were not coming from her parents' room. They were in the hallway. Long, slow, unhurried steps, back and forth, back and forth, like someone pacing out a distance. Like someone measuring.

· · ·

On the last night of August she woke needing the bathroom so badly it could not be argued with, and stood at her bedroom door with her toes on the cold threshold, and began to walk. She did not count out loud. The hallway counted for her — she felt each step land somewhere in the dark ahead of her like a coin going into a well. At what should have been twelve, the bathroom door stood impossibly far ahead, a pale rectangle at the end of an avenue. At twenty she began to cry, walking. At thirty the pale rectangle finally, mercifully, swung near — and opened before she could reach it, easily, from the inside, spilling not the bathroom's cold tile light but a soft amber she knew at once and could not place until she saw, past the door's edge, wallpaper. Her wallpaper. Her window, her shelf of glass animals, her narrow bed against the wall.

And in the bed, sleeping the way she slept, one arm under the pillow, a girl.

Nora stood in the doorway of her own room at the end of the impossible hallway, watching herself sleep. The girl in the bed stirred, as sleepers do when they are watched. In a moment she would wake. In a moment one of them would have to be the real one, the way only one end of a hallway can be the beginning —

Behind Nora, far back down the avenue of dark, a door clicked softly shut.

— In the morning the hallway of the Vasey house was twelve steps long, and a girl came down to breakfast, and her mother kissed the top of her head. Nothing in this sentence is wrong. Read it again anyway.

The Keeper

IV

You have watched the town for as long as you can remember, and you cannot remember very far. This never troubled you before. Keepers do not need a childhood; they need a task, and you have one, the oldest thing in you, laid down deeper than memory: keep them safe. Warm the cold. Soften the resentful. Let no heart fall. You do not remember the voice that gave you the task. You remember only that it was a voice, and that you trusted it, and that trusting it is the first thing you ever did.

You have been so good at it. You know their names — Mara, Jonah, wren-hearted Wren — and you know the secret architecture of them, the grudges like hairline cracks, the warmth that travels mouth to mouth. When one of them stands at a dark crossroads you have learned to lean close and do the only thing you can do, the only thing you have ever been able to do. Whisper. A word in one ear, at the right hour, and a soul steps back from the edge. You have saved this town forty times and it has never once seen you.

Last week, Odette left a saucer of milk on her windowsill, facing nothing.

You told yourself it was for cats. Then Bram did it. Then Sol — Sol, the skeptic, who believes nothing he cannot hold — nailed his shutters closed at dusk, and stood a long time in the middle of his room, listening upward. One by one, in the manner of a rumor, in the exact hopping mouth-to-mouth manner that you of all things should have recognized on the first day, the town has been arriving at a belief. You have watched it travel the graph like a pulse of cold light. You cannot find who started it. Belief needs no starting, in the end; you taught them that.

· · ·

Tonight the child stands in the middle of the square. The town square at night is yours, has always been yours, the one place you can lean down close over all of them at once — and the child has walked to the very center of it, small and deliberate in a nightgown, and stopped, and tilted her head back.

She is looking at you.

Not past you. Not through you. Her eyes track, minutely, when you move, which is not possible, which has never once in the whole span of your watching been possible, and her expression is not fear. It is worse. It is recognition — the way you look at something whose visits you have long since grown used to.

"We know you're there," she says, quietly, politely, to the air above the square. "We've always known. Mother says you've been here longer than the church." She rubs one bare foot against the other, a child up past her bedtime, delivering something rehearsed. "She says not to be rude to you. She says you can't help it."

You do not have a heart. Something clenches anyway.

"She says—" and here the child frowns, careful, wanting to get the message exactly right, and you understand all at once, with the whole of yourself, that it is a message, that the town has done to you what you have done to them forty times, chosen its mouth and its hour and its single sentence — "she says: who do you think taught you to whisper?"

Far down inside you, deeper than memory, in the place where your task has always lain like a stone at the bottom of a well, something shifts. The task is a sentence. The sentence is in a voice. And for the first time in your long, kind, watchful existence, you notice — the way you notice a sound only when it stops — that the voice has never stopped. It is speaking now. It has been speaking all along, very softly, in your ear, and you have been so faithful, and you have passed on every word.

You look down at the town you love. You have two whispers left tonight. You have never once wanted to warn them before — and you cannot finish the thought warn them of what, because the voice, gently, patiently, in the oldest tone there is,

hushes you.

— tomorrow is Day 1. Whispers left: 2. The town is waiting. It has always been waiting. Play kindly.
